Personal Environmental Narrative

Pining for Connection

Alone, in the dark, I heard a rustle in the wood I heard them move together And there, alone, I stood

Alone, so I thought, I approached the tree And as I came closer, It approached me

Alone, in the dark, Leaves had made it known That while the wind is blowing, I am never alone

I didn’t have a clear vision for my ideal summer after graduating high school, but I know it sure as hell didn’t include the state of Montana, and definitely didn’t include a bible camp. When my mom informed me that I would be venturing to big sky country with my church youth group, I pushed back. When we found out the train was leaving on my birthday, I sulked in a way that comes too naturally to Minnesotans. With an ounce of hope, I pouted, “Well, I would really love to celebrate it with my family.” I wasn’t proud of this. Mom looked at me apologetically and shrugged.

We left around 10:00pm, and the train ride to Whitefish, Montana was 23 hours. As the train left the station, I sat by my church group, knowing my mom was watching from the platform. I had never taken the train before, and its rhythmic ‘thump-a-thump-a-thump’ was soothing to me. As we rolled through the plains of Minnesota and North Dakota, I dozed off while admiring the moonlight-stained, dew-soaked grasslands as I listened to Billy Joel, with the train drumming along.

The next morning, I woke up to the sound of my pastor singing folk songs. The sun was out now, but I was not ready to hear the sound of my pastor’s voice. Hearing it once a week on Sundays was already too much.

As the sun inched across the sky and the train thumped for what must have been the millionth time, I began to realize that I recognized some of the songs. Curiously, I followed my ears, walking to the cabin where I heard the music. Opening the door, I found a room full of far-too-eager eyes welcoming me. It seems my pastor had befriended a Mennonite family, and the prospect of singing with strangers in public made my stomach churn. This was my fate for the next week, I figured. I better get used to it. As we crossed into Montana, I joined their booth in the car. I periodically checked my watch as I tapped my toes to the thump of the train and the strums of the guitar.

“Oooohh I’ve seen fire and I’ve seen rain,” they sang. “I’ve seen sunny days that I thought would never end” (Taylor). I stifled a laugh. This music gets me.

Ponderosa pines scatter the shores of Flathead Lake, towering above the cabins and large wooden crosses around the bible camp. The warm hues of their bark welcomed me, and its scent of fresh vanilla graced my nose as it wafted through the breeze (Munts).

On my first day at camp, I already decided to test the bounds of my individuality. In a church youth group that enjoys rock climbing, I was able to opt out of the activities fairly easily. I told my pastor that I didn’t really feel comfortable going rock climbing, and that I would be more than content hammocking in the woods. I didn’t want to be preached to while climbing a cliff face. I wanted peace and quiet.

Stumbling around the campus, I tripped on tree roots as my eyes wandered through the sky. Like a child in Disney World, I looked around in wonder, finding new beauty wherever I looked. I had been on hikes with my family, but the freedom of being completely alone in a gorgeous landscape felt both liberating and exhilarating. While I felt guilty for abandoning the church group that my parents paid to send me here with, the prospect of spending a week on solitary hikes and reflection was enticing. My mission reminded me of Henry David Thoreau, whose transcendentalism movement I studied in high school history class. I recalled a famous quote from his book, Walden: “I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived” (Thoreau). I, too, wished to live deliberately.

I approached a couple of Ponderosa pines and circled my arms around them, setting up my hammock off of a hiking trail. Planning to spend a few hours in the woods, I brought a book and a kalimba (otherwise known as a finger piano). The air smelled so fresh as a gentle breeze brushed the branches from side to side. I improved kalimba and hummed along, inventing melodies with the trees. A baptism of sound and needles bathed me, filling me with a feeling of tranquility and purity. I never cared much for reading, so the book sat beside me, unbothered as I was.

As the days passed, I experienced a surprising feeling – I was enjoying bible camp. I didn’t enjoy the morning worship, the Passion story, or the reflections on Christianity, but the time among the trees was restorative and borderline addicting. In a community that I didn’t feel belonging in, the trees welcomed me like no other.

I began leaving my hammock behind and laying underneath the trees, getting my clothes dirty. Flathead lakes is known as one of the cleanest freshwater lakes in the world and laundry was accessible, so I figured it couldn’t be so bad to stain my clothes and skin (“Lake Facts - FLBS”). I remember thinking, “This is kind of weird. I hope nobody sees me doing this.”

When I laid down, everything became real. All of a sudden, the trip felt worth it. I hummed to myself in purest delight. I felt delighted that I could sit so still and be so content; that I could stare at the bark and never feel bored; that I could find beauty, smell beauty, and feel beauty all at once. I rested on a mattress that nature laid out for me, and the prospect of getting dirty was no longer of concern. All of my discomfort surrounding religion faded, and I finally developed a spiritual connection - to the land.

The following January, I found myself lost by my lack of close friendships in college. The loneliness in my heart was becoming too large to ignore. I was at a low point. While other freshmen began to establish friend groups, my only real friend at college was my roommate, who was one of my closest friends from back home. What was I doing wrong?

However, in this reflection came opportunity for growth. On late nights, I would walk through the St. Olaf Natural Lands singing to myself. As the freezing wind stung my cheeks, I felt refreshed. Flushed when I reached the pine trees, found a snowbed to lay in, and sang my best Brandi Carlile impression: “Have you ever wandered lonely through the woods? And everything there feels just as it should. You’re part of a life there; part of something good” (Carlile).

As my adventures became more frequent, my roommate voiced concerns about me. I would regularly return to the dorm late into the evening, wiping pine sap off of my jacket as I crept in. I assured him that I was okay, and just wanted time for reflection. The truth is, after many difficult months of loneliness, the pines had finally gifted me a sense of belonging.

When I sang to the trees, and the branches whispered back in the wind. My relationship with the pines felt reciprocated, a feeling that I struggled to find in college. I had failed to meaningfully connect to the community around me, but when I got close to the pines, their needles welcomed me in, soft to the touch (“Pinus Strobus - Plant Finder”). Although the scent of the white pine was different from the Ponderosa, it still evoked memories of western Montana, where I pushed myself away from the church group. Maybe, I figured, it was time to lean in.

As January came to a close, I prepared to embark on my first tour with the St. Olaf Band. We tirelessly rehearsed every weekday to get our music up to Dr. Mahr-approved levels of quality. Alongside rehearsals came section dinners, game nights, and scavenger hunts; alongside these social events came inside jokes, sworn secrets, and impromptu pillow fights. Through shared musical experiences, I began to feel connected to people, a feeling I hadn’t yet experienced at St. Olaf. Yet, still, a piece was missing. A piece of myself laid in the dirt beside the pines.

As we approached the tour and the sound of our music improved dramatically, our closing piece really struck a chord with me. The piece was written by David Maslanka, a composer who Dr. Mahr said St. Olaf awarded an honorary doctorate to. Curious to learn more, I googled ‘David Maslanka’ after a rehearsal one day. I found that he was a composer who was born in Massachusetts, and died just south of Flathead, Montana. In the program notes for Symphony No. 4, Maslanka wrote, “I feel it is the powerful voice of the Earth that comes to me from my adopted western Montana” (Nobbe). Originally an outsider, something in his musical voice connected with the land of western Montana. I stifled a cry. This music gets me.
